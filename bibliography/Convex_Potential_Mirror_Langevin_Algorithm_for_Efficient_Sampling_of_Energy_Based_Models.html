Convex Potential Mirror Langevin Algorithm for Efficient Sampling of Energy-Based Models

Yang Zitao , Amin Ullah , Shuai Li , Fuxin Li and Jun Li

The Thirty-Ninth Annual Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems, 2025

In short

Sampling from an energy-based model with Langevin dynamics gets stuck in local energy minima because the sampler moves through Euclidean space while the data lives on a curved manifold. Mirror Langevin methods fix the geometry with a fixed, hand-picked mirror map. CPMLA instead learns the mirror map from data as the gradient of a convex potential, and trains it jointly with the EBM. It beats CoopFlow on CIFAR-10 FID (20.85 vs 21.16) using 20 sampling steps instead of 30 — and 0.9% of the flow parameters.

Problem and motivation

An energy-based model defines a density as \(p_\theta(x) = \frac{1}{Z(\theta)}\exp[f_\theta(x)]\) — low energy for likely data, high energy for unlikely. This is appealing because it demands almost nothing architecturally: you specify an unnormalised energy function and nothing else. No invertibility requirement, no tractable noise process. That flexibility is why EBMs turn up in 3D object recognition, image segmentation, super-resolution, machine translation and protein folding.

The cost is sampling. Because \(Z(\theta)\) is intractable, training and generation both need MCMC, and in high dimensions Langevin dynamics runs into two failure modes: it gets trapped in local minima when the energy landscape is multi-modal, and it treats the space as Euclidean when the data manifold is not. The result is expensive, biased sampling and slow convergence.

Mirror Langevin algorithms address the geometry directly. By running the dynamics in a dual space defined by a mirror map, they achieve vanishing bias as step size goes to zero and mixing times independent of the domain’s condition number. But the mirror map is fixed — a predefined function chosen in advance. For the complex manifolds that deep EBMs are meant to model, no hand-picked map fits, which has kept these methods off large-scale problems.

Key idea

Learn the mirror map instead of choosing it.

The map is parameterised as the gradient of a convex potential, following a CP-Flow. This is not an arbitrary choice: by Brenier’s theorem in optimal transport, the gradient of a convex potential is exactly the structure of an optimal transport map between distributions, which guarantees the learned map captures the geometry of the data distribution rather than merely being flexible.

Because the map is learned from data, it reorients the sampling trajectory to follow the manifold’s actual non-Euclidean geometry — exploring high-density regions while avoiding the metastable states where Euclidean Langevin dynamics stalls.

The second idea is cooperative training. The CP-Flow and the EBM improve each other: the CP-Flow is trained on both real and synthesised examples, then the EBM is updated by contrasting real samples against those CPMLA generated. The flow’s transport dynamics and the EBM’s density estimate converge together, which both reduces sampling bias and speeds convergence.

How the algorithm works

Each CPMLA iteration has three stages:

  1. Initialise in the dual space. Noise examples are drawn from a standard Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\) directly in the dual space — so no initial forward mapping is needed.
  2. Run T mirror Langevin steps. In the dual space, alternate a gradient step on the EBM energy with an SDE noise step, with noise drawn from \(\mathcal{N}(0, \nabla^2 G_\vartheta(x_k))\).
  3. Map back. The inverse map \(\nabla G^*_\vartheta\) returns samples to the primal space.

Two computational choices make this tractable. The expensive matrix square root of the Hessian is avoided by noting that \(\sqrt{2\eta \nabla^2 G_\vartheta(x)}\,\tilde\xi_k\) with standard Gaussian noise is statistically equivalent to \(\sqrt{2\eta}\,\xi_k\) with noise drawn from the Hessian-covariance Gaussian. And the Hessian is approximated by its diagonal, dropping complexity from \(O(d^3)\) to \(O(d)\) — the step that makes high-dimensional sampling feasible at all. Successive iterations also let \(\nabla G^*_\vartheta\) and \(\nabla G_\vartheta\) cancel, since they are inverses.

The MCMC chain is re-initialised at every update rather than persisted, following Persistent Contrastive Divergence practice, so gradients are estimated from the current model distribution rather than stale samples.

Theoretical analysis

The paper proves exponential convergence with vanishing bias under relaxed log-concavity, and describes this as the first convergence analysis of mirror Langevin algorithms where the mirror map is a deep neural network.

Two extensions over prior analysis: the proof is specialised to a neural-network mirror map, and it accounts for the approximation error of both the CP-Flow and the EBM, not just the sampling algorithm’s own error.

The assumptions are handled with unusual care. Assumption 4.1 (β-Mirror Log-Sobolev Inequality) is acknowledged as unverifiable for real high-dimensional data — the paper states plainly that it is standard and necessary in this literature, and that its own contribution concerns what can be controlled and verified. Assumption 4.2 (ζ-self-concordance, bounding the third derivative relative to the second so the geometry cannot change too abruptly) is empirically validated on CIFAR-10: since computing the third-derivative tensor directly is infeasible, Frobenius norms of the Hessian and of five random directional third derivatives are estimated with Hutchinson’s estimator via Hessian-vector products, giving a proxy for ζ.

Experiments

Toy study. On the eight-Gaussians density, CPMLA matches in 3 iterations what CP-Flow needs 10 iterations to reach.

Image generation. Evaluated on CIFAR-10, SVHN and CelebA, all resized to 32x32, with FID over 50,000 samples. Two settings are reported: CPMLA (both components from scratch) and CPMLA-prt (CP-Flow pretrained on data first, then used to initialise).

On CIFAR-10, CPMLA-prt reaches 20.85 at T=20 and CPMLA 21.09 at T=30, against CoopFlow’s 30.74 at T=20 and 21.16 at T=30. Against Flow+EBM baselines the gap is large — NT-EBM sits at 78.12 and EBM-FCE at 37.30.

The paper is explicit that beating score-based diffusion is not the goal. NCSN++ reaches 2.20 and StyleGAN2-ADA 2.92, both far ahead. The argument is that EBMs buy modelling flexibility — you only specify an unnormalised energy — and that this work narrows the sample-quality gap enough to make that flexibility practical.

Efficiency is where the result is strongest:

  • Lower FID than CoopFlow with fewer sampling steps (20 vs 30) and less wall-clock time (15.92 vs 16.84 s per 1k images).
  • The flow component uses 0.26M parameters against CoopFlow’s 28.78M — 0.9% — while the EBM part is identical at 17.13M.
  • Training within a 24GB VRAM budget takes 10.5 hours versus roughly 38 for CoopFlow. Per-iteration CPMLA is actually slower (15.7 s/iter vs 12.0), because CP-Flow is a more complex architecture; the advantage comes entirely from memory efficiency permitting larger batches.

Reconstruction and inpainting. CPMLA reconstructs CIFAR-10 test images with high fidelity, and inpaints masked CelebA faces by optimising an energy that measures difference over the masked region while constraining undamaged pixels to stay fixed.

Key contributions

  • Introduced CPMLA, which replaces the fixed mirror map of conventional mirror Langevin algorithms with a learnable one parameterised as the gradient of a convex potential, justified by Brenier’s theorem.
  • Trained the mirror map and the EBM cooperatively, so the flow’s transport dynamics and the energy-based density estimate improve each other rather than being fit independently.
  • Gave the first convergence analysis of mirror Langevin algorithms with deep-network mirror maps, proving exponential convergence with vanishing bias while accounting for the approximation error of both learned components.
  • Achieved better sample quality than CoopFlow with fewer sampling iterations, less inference time, and 0.9% of the flow parameter count.

Datasets

  • Eight Gaussians (toy) — Synthetic multi-modal density used to show convergence speed against CP-Flow.
  • CIFAR-10 — 50,000 training and 10,000 test images across 10 categories; main FID benchmark.
  • SVHN — Over 70,000 training and 20,000 test images of house numbers.
  • CelebA — Over 200,000 celebrity face images; also used for the inpainting demonstration.

Limitations and open questions

  • The β-Mirror LSI assumption underpinning the convergence proof cannot be verified for real high-dimensional data; the paper states this directly rather than claiming otherwise.
  • The Hessian of the learned potential is approximated by its diagonal to make sampling affordable, discarding the off-diagonal curvature the mirror map was introduced to capture.
  • FID remains an order of magnitude behind score-based diffusion (20.85 vs 2.20 for NCSN++). The contribution is efficiency within the EBM family, not competitive image quality overall.
  • Per-iteration training is slower than CoopFlow; the reported speed advantage depends on memory headroom allowing larger batches, so it is hardware-dependent.
  • All image experiments are at 32x32 resolution, leaving scaling behaviour untested.
  • The best FID comes from the pretrained variant (CPMLA-prt), so the strongest result depends on a separate CP-Flow pretraining stage rather than fully joint training from scratch.
